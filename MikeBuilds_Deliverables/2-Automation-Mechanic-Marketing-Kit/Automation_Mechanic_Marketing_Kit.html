Section 01 · Lane A, stage two
The Automation Mechanic menu

Four products. Each one has a real name a tradesman can repeat to his wife at dinner, a price he can say out loud, and a pain he already feels. No tech words anywhere — “n8n” will never come out of your mouth on a sales call.

Positioning line for the whole menu, usable anywhere: “Your back office, while you’re on a job.” That’s it. That’s the brand. Everything below hangs off that one sentence.

01  The Missed-Call Texter $400 once
For: any one-man crew whose phone rings while both hands are busy

The pain. Every call he can’t take is a customer calling the next name on Google. Sixty seconds. That’s all it takes to hand a $400 job to a competitor. He knows it happens. He can’t quantify it, which makes it worse.

The fix. The moment a call goes unanswered, the customer gets a text within ten seconds: “Hi, this is Dave’s Plumbing — we’re on a job right now. Can we call you back in 20?” The customer feels seen. They wait. Dave books the job he would’ve lost.

Line to remember: “It texts every missed caller back before they finish dialing the next guy.”
02  The Review Robot $300 once
For: anyone with great Google reviews and zero replies

The pain. Forty glowing reviews, not one response. To a customer scanning the list, that reads as “this shop went quiet.” Future buyers notice. It quietly costs him jobs every week and he has no idea.

The fix. Every new review gets a warm, personal reply within the hour — at 2 a.m., while he’s under a sink, on Thanksgiving. Five-star reviews get gratitude. Three-star reviews get a professional answer that shows everyone else watching how he handles problems. That second part is the part that sells the next customer.

Line to remember: “Your reviews stop looking abandoned. Reply #41 makes you look open for business.”
03  The Invoice Chaser $300–500 once
For: anyone who hates the money call more than the crawl space

The pain. Nobody became a plumber to chase checks. So invoices sit. Thirty, sixty, ninety days. The awkward call gets postponed forever, and the “good guy” reflex quietly bleeds the business dry.

The fix. Polite, escalating reminders that run themselves. Day 3, a friendly nudge. Day 10, firmer. Day 20, a final notice. The owner stays the good guy; the robot plays bad cop. Most small businesses collect tens of thousands in old invoices within the first month of running one of these.

Line to remember: “You never make the awkward call again. The robot makes it for you, politely, forever.”
04  The 24/7 Receptionist $297 / month
For: the crew that’s outgrown voicemail

The pain. After-hours calls hit voicemail. Eight in ten callers just dial the next company instead of leaving a message. He knows it. He’s tired. The phone rings while he’s eating dinner with his kids.

The fix. A friendly voice answers every call, any hour. It answers basic questions with his rules — the service-call fee, the service area, the emergency line. It books them on his calendar. This is the only monthly product on the menu, and that’s the pitch: one saved job a month covers her salary.

Line to remember: “She answers every call you miss, forever, for less than a phone bill.”

A note on the pricing: these match the ranges the playbook research validated ($300–600 for local automation builds, $297/mo for an AI front desk). Hold your prices. A $300 “yes” is cheaper than a free “maybe” that eats three weeks of evenings. If someone haggles, drop scope before you drop price — cut the escalation steps out of the Invoice Chaser, don’t cut $100 off the top.

Section 04 · Lane B, the storefront
The Fiverr Gig Ladder

Three listings, publishable tonight. Written for buyers who have never heard of you, never heard of n8n, and don’t care. They care about the hour of their day you’re giving back.

One deliberate exception to plain English: the gig titles carry the search word (“n8n”) because that’s literally what buyers type into Fiverr’s search bar. Everything after the title — description, FAQs, packages — speaks human. That’s the balance: searchable where people search, plain where people read.

Platform rules — the cheat sheet
  • Title: 80 characters max. Keyword goes first, not last.
  • Description: 1,200 characters max. The first two lines show before “see more” — spend them on the pain, never on a greeting.
  • Tags: 5 tags, 20 characters each. Given per gig below.
  • Packages: 3 tiers. Most orders land in the middle tier — make it the obvious choice.
  • Images: up to 3, at 1272×762px. One bold sentence per image, before/after style. No walls of text.
  • Video: a 60–75 second screen recording lifts clicks noticeably. Record it on day one, not “later.”
  • The wall rule: no external links, no phone numbers, no emails anywhere in a gig. Fiverr suspends accounts for it. Everything stays on-platform until delivery is done.
New-account warm-up

Fill every profile field the first night — empty fields read as “risk.” Keep the Fiverr app open on your phone in the evenings; response time is a ranking factor and a new seller’s only weapon. Answer every message within the hour. Expect the scraping gig to convert first (steady demand, fast delivery) — deliver those orders in under 24 hours and let the reviews stack while the bigger n8n tickets find you.

The proposal formula — five lines, never more

The 5-line structure
  1. Mirror their words. Quote one line from their job post back at them. It proves you read it.
  2. The fix in one sentence. Plain English. No feature lists.
  3. One line of proof. A specific, similar build. “Same bones” is the phrase that does the work.
  4. Price and days. Firm numbers. Confidence reads as competence.
  5. One smart question. It starts the conversation before they’ve even hired you.

Section 07 · The honest verdicts
Substack, Gumroad — and what to run instead

You asked whether those two platforms fit this play. Straight answer, no hedging: not this quarter. Here’s the reasoning, what replaces each, and exactly when the door reopens.

Substack — skip it

Newsletter readers are other builders, writers, and marketers — not people with plumbing emergencies and $400 checks. A newsletter pays in 6 to 12 months of consistency. Your clock says eight weeks. Publishing weekly would also eat the exact writing hours that should go into gig descriptions and proposals, which reach actual buyers this month. Wrong audience, wrong clock. That’s the whole verdict.

The writing habit doesn’t die, though — it moves. Every week you’ll already be writing one Instagram caption and two proposals. That’s the same muscle, aimed at people who can pay you now. Revisit Substack in January as the content arm of the 2027 portfolio, when you’re selling to builders instead of tradesmen.

Gumroad — not yet, by your own rule

Section 09 · Apprentice training
Three drills before you publish

Marketing material only works when it sounds like you saying it, not a document agreeing with you. These three drills put the voice in your mouth. Fifteen minutes each, done out loud, not in your head.

Drill 1 · De-slop the sentence

Read this out loud — it’s the kind of sentence that gets gigs skipped:

“I will utilize premium end-to-end automation solutions engineered for your enterprise requirements.”

Your turn: rewrite it so a roofer would nod. Fix it for meaning first, then say your version three times out loud until it’s yours.

One fix: “Tell me the task that eats your hour. I’ll make it cost zero.” Same offer. Ninety percent fewer words. That gap between the two sentences is the entire difference between marketing that gets read and marketing that gets scrolled.

Drill 2 · The Dave replay, stage two

Dave bought the website Tuesday. It’s Friday, and you just sent Script A. He replies:

“how does it even work? I miss calls all day lol”

Your turn: draft the next text. Rules: under 30 words, no tech words, one question at the end.

One fix: “next call you miss, watch your phone — the customer gets a text from you in 10 seconds, before they can call anyone else. want me to run it for a week free so you can see it fire?” A free week is a $400 sale with training wheels. He watches it save a job, then he pays.

Drill 3 · The 90-second video

Every offer in this kit ends in the same place: a demo video. So record the template one — a fake plumbing company, one take, phone camera on the screen, no editing:

  • 0–5s: “This is what happens when a plumber misses a call.” Show the ringing phone. Let it ring out. Silence is the hook.
  • 5–15s: The money math on screen: “one lost job pays for this twice.”
  • 15–60s: The demo. The text lands. The customer replies. The calendar fills. Narrate plainly, no excitement — deadpan sells machinery.
  • 60–75s: “$400, it’s yours, no monthly fee. Text me.” Phone number on screen, twice.

This video is the workhorse: it closes texts, fills the Instagram bio link, and becomes the Fiverr gig video with the number cut out. Record it once, use it everywhere. Bad first take beats perfect never-recorded — every time.
